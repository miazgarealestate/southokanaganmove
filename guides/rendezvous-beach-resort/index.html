Rendezvous Beach Resort: buying a leasehold on reserve land

The short version: on reserve land you buy a lease, not title. That changes financing, resale and what you should ask before a deposit.

Sources checked 12 November 2026. General guidance, not legal, lending or tax advice. Have a lawyer experienced in First Nations land review any lease before you pay a deposit.

What the public record shows

  • A federal impact-assessment registry entry (reference 86105) describes a proposed Rendezvous Beach Resort with the Osoyoos Indian Band as proponent and Rendezvous Beach Resort Ltd. named as proposing the project. It is an RV resort and campground with an estimated 200 RV sites on about 14.1 hectares of Lot 396, Osoyoos Indian Reserve No. 1, south of Oliver on Osoyoos Lake. Indigenous Services Canada is the assessing authority; the entry shows the review opened on 21 November 2023. Impact Assessment Agency registry.
  • MLS® listings under the name Rendezvous RV Resort, 2501 Radio Tower Road, have described finished lakefront units built in 2023 on a clubhouse-and-pool site, with listing prices of $699,000 and $749,000 on the listings reviewed (per MLS® listings; listing dates and current status not confirmed here).
  • In the MLS® export Pat's team reviewed on 9 October 2026, the only active listing naming Rendezvous was Unit 17 at Rendezvous RV Resort, listed at $199,000 (per MLS® listing). Check current availability, because this changes.

Not confirmed: whether the registry's “Rendezvous Beach Resort” and the “Rendezvous RV Resort” at 2501 Radio Tower Road are the same development, which phases are built or for sale, and the terms of any site lease. Public sources found do not settle these points, so confirm them in writing with the developer and in the lease itself.

Why a lease is a different purchase

  • Title to reserve land is held by the Crown. The interest normally available to a non-member is a leasehold, and before a lease can be granted the First Nation must designate the land under the Indian Act. See McCarthy Tétrault: title to First Nations land in BC.
  • Reserve land is not registered in a provincial Land Title Office. Interests are recorded in a federal or First Nation registry, so the usual title search does not tell the whole story.
  • The Indian Act generally bars mortgages on reserve property, but allows a mortgage of a leasehold interest on designated land. Lenders differ in whether and how they lend against one. See Ratcliff & Company overview and CMHC on leasehold security.

Questions to ask before a deposit

  1. Who is the landlord and lessor, and is the land designated for leasing? Ask to see the head lease as well as your sublease.
  2. How long is the term, can it be renewed, and what happens to the structure at the end?
  3. What are the lease payments now, how do they change (for example, by an index), and what one-time fees apply and what do they cover?
  4. Does the lease allow resale, and does a buyer need the landlord's or the Minister's consent? What does that process cost?
  5. What are the build, size, use and rental rules for your site, and who approves plans?
  6. Who provides water, sewer, power, roads and insurance, and who pays for upkeep and future upgrades?
  7. Will your lender finance this specific lease? Ask before you sign, not after.
  8. Which property taxes or lease-equivalent charges apply, and who collects them?

Pat's building and renovation background helps on the construction side: what a structure on a site like this should include, and what a finished unit's condition tells you. The legal and financing answers still have to come from your lawyer and lender.
